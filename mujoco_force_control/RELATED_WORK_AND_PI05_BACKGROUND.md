# Related Work and π₀.₅ Background

## Related Work

**Classical Robot Control and Manipulation Primitives**

The decomposition of complex manipulation tasks into reusable primitives has deep roots in robotics. Hogan's seminal work on impedance control established that robots should regulate dynamic behavior (spring-damper relationships) rather than tracking rigid trajectories, enabling compliant interaction with constrained environments. Hybrid position/force control, introduced by Raibert and Craig, enabled tasks like deburring and assembly where some axes must track positions while others track forces—a framework we directly employ in our PUSH primitive. These classical approaches are interpretable, provably stable under certain conditions, and remain the backbone of industrial robot programming. However, they require manual tuning of control gains for each task, robot, and environment, limiting generalization. Our work builds on these foundations by learning to orchestrate such primitives from demonstrations rather than hand-coding each application.

**Imitation Learning and Behavioral Cloning**

Learning from demonstrations has been a central theme in robotics since Pomerleau's ALVINN system (1989), which learned to drive a road-following vehicle by imitating human drivers. Behavioral cloning—the supervised learning approach we use here—frames imitation as supervised regression: given observation-action pairs from an expert, minimize prediction error on expert actions. While simple and data-efficient compared to reinforcement learning, behavioral cloning suffers from distribution shift: if the learned policy makes a prediction slightly different from the expert, subsequent observations diverge from the training distribution, causing compounding errors. Abbeel and Ng's apprenticeship learning addressed this by inferring reward functions from demonstrations, enabling RL agents to optimize beyond expert performance. More recent work by Ross et al. (Dataset Aggregation, DAgger) tackled distribution shift by actively collecting data from the learned policy's trajectories and re-labeling with the expert. For our ball-in-cup task, we employ pure behavioral cloning as a baseline, which is appropriate because the hand-coded controller generates highly consistent, low-error trajectories that leave little room for distribution shift over short time horizons (5-second tasks). Future work could incorporate DAgger or online learning to handle longer-horizon tasks.

**Vision-Language Models and Grounding**

The rise of large language models (GPT-3, PaLM) and vision-language models (CLIP, ALIGN) has revolutionized multi-modal understanding. CLIP demonstrated that contrastive learning on image-text pairs from the internet can produce embeddings where semantic similarity is preserved across modalities: similar images and similar text descriptions map to nearby points in embedding space. This pre-trained representation can be adapted to robotics by using CLIP to embed task language (e.g., "push gently") and conditioning robot policies on these embeddings. Recent VLA models have extended this to action prediction: RT-1 (Robotics Transformer) by Brohan et al. combined a vision transformer backbone with action tokenization to map observations and natural language to discrete robot actions, trained on 130K trajectories from a real robot. RT-2 extended this with large language models for reasoning. These models show that pre-training on diverse, large-scale data enables better generalization and zero-shot transfer. Our use of CLIP embeddings for language conditioning follows this paradigm, though at a smaller scale appropriate for single-task validation.

**Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Foundation Models**

The term "VLA" (Vision-Language-Action) refers to foundation models that jointly process visual observations, language instructions, and proprioceptive state to output robot actions. π₀.₅ by Physical Intelligence represents a recent advance in this space, demonstrating that a single model can perform 50+ diverse manipulation tasks (pick, place, insert, poke, etc.) when pre-trained on large datasets and fine-tuned with task-specific demonstrations. Prior VLA work includes PaLM-E (combining PaLM language models with embodied action prediction) and Gato (a general agent trained on text, images, and game/robot trajectories). A key insight from this literature is that multimodal pre-training (learning rich representations from diverse data) enables better transfer and few-shot adaptation than single-task training from scratch. Our project validates whether this principle holds for learning task-oriented behavior from hand-coded demonstrations: can a VLA model, even when trained on a small single-task dataset, outperform simpler baselines and capture language-conditioned behavior?

**Sim-to-Real Transfer and Domain Randomization**

A critical challenge in robotic learning is that simulators are imperfect: visual appearance differs (lighting, textures, camera calibration), contact dynamics are simplified (rigid body vs. deformable), and actuators have latency and nonlinearities. Sim-to-real gap research has produced several mitigation strategies. Domain randomization (Tobin et al., Sim2Real) randomly varies visual and dynamics parameters during training, forcing the policy to be robust to domain shift; the hope is that training on diverse simulated domains includes the real domain as a sample. Other approaches use adversarial training (find worst-case domain shifts and train against them), or learn domain-invariant representations via self-supervised learning. Our initial work focuses on validation in clean simulation; future phases will systematically add noise and randomization to characterize and close the sim-to-real gap. We note that ball-in-cup is a good benchmark for this because contact-rich manipulation is particularly sensitive to dynamics mismatches.

**Manipulation Benchmarks and Task Design**

Designing representative benchmarks is crucial for progress. In computer vision, ImageNet standardized evaluation; in language, GLUE and SuperGLUE provide diverse tasks. For robotic manipulation, several benchmarks have emerged: YCBK object dataset with industry-standard benchmark objects, RoboBench (diverse simulated tasks), and real-robot benchmarks like those from Google Brain (630 pick-and-place trials) and DeepMind. Ball-in-cup is a classic control problem with pedagogical value (understanding the nonlinear dynamics teaches important lessons) and practical relevance (similar reaching, pushing, and aiming strategies appear in assembly, bin-picking, and object placement). Our choice to use ball-in-cup as the validation task reflects a pragmatic middle ground: more complex than reaching, more tractable than full assembly sequences.

## Understanding π₀.₅: Vision-Language-Action for Robotics

### What is π₀.₅?

π₀.₅ is a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation model developed by Physical Intelligence for general-purpose robotic manipulation. Unlike traditional robot controllers (which process sensor inputs and output action commands), π₀.₅ jointly processes three types of input—visual observations (RGB images from robot cameras), natural language instructions (task descriptions from humans), and proprioceptive state (joint positions, velocities, forces)—and predicts the next robot action. The model is pre-trained on a large, diverse dataset of robot trajectories (collected from multiple robot embodiments, tasks, and environments) and can be fine-tuned on specific tasks with as few as 50–100 demonstrations. The name "π" (pi) reflects the mathematical precision of robotics; "0.5" indicates it is a pre-release version, part of Physical Intelligence's iterative approach to embodied AI.

The defining characteristic of π₀.₅ is its **multimodal fusion**: it learns to combine information from vision (where things are, what they look like), language (what the task intent is), and proprioception (what the robot's current state is) into a unified representation, then decodes this representation into actions. This is fundamentally different from classical robot programming, where each modality is processed separately and language is optional. By treating modalities symmetrically, π₀.₅ can leverage pre-trained vision and language models (e.g., CLIP, Vision Transformers, GPT) as components, inheriting their generalization capabilities.

### Architecture Overview

π₀.₅ follows a transformer-based architecture, the dominant paradigm in modern deep learning for handling sequential and structured data. The overall pipeline can be conceptualized in three stages: encoding, fusion, and decoding.

**Encoding Stage:** Raw inputs are converted into fixed-dimensional representations. The vision input (a sequence of RGB images, typically 224×224 pixels at 10 Hz from the robot's camera) is processed by a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone, which divides each image into 16×16 pixel patches, embeds them, and applies transformer layers to capture spatial relationships and context. The output is a sequence of token embeddings, one per patch, plus a class token that summarizes the entire image. These tokens live in a high-dimensional space (e.g., 1024 dimensions for π₀.₅'s large variant) and can be thought of as "visual concepts" learned from pre-training.

Proprioceptive state (joint positions, velocities, forces, gripper state—typically 20–30 scalar values) is encoded via simple linear embeddings followed by positional encoding, converting scalars into tokens in the same embedding space as vision. This allows the model to treat sensors uniformly: whether information comes from vision or force feedback, it is represented as tokens that the transformer can attend over.

Natural language instructions (e.g., "pick up the blue ball and place it in the bin gently") are tokenized and processed by a pre-trained text encoder, typically CLIP's text tower or GPT-style language models. The language encoder produces token embeddings that align with the visual embedding space due to pre-training on image-caption pairs. This cross-modal alignment is crucial: "pick up" in language space points toward visual patterns (gripper approaching object) and action patterns (opening gripper → moving → closing gripper).

**Fusion Stage:** Tokens from vision, proprioception, and language are concatenated into a single sequence and processed by transformer layers dedicated to multimodal interaction. Each transformer layer contains self-attention (within each modality and across modalities) and cross-attention blocks, allowing the model to reason about relationships: "the word 'gently' should reduce force; I see the ball is near the cup in the image; my current joint position is θ; therefore, I should command a reduced force." These layers are typically trained end-to-end on robot trajectories, learning task-specific attention patterns that the pre-trained encoders (vision, language) did not capture.

**Decoding Stage:** The final transformer layer's hidden state (usually an aggregated representation summing information across modalities and time) is fed through action heads that predict the robot's next action. Depending on the robot and task, actions can be represented as: continuous joint targets (our choice for ball-in-cup), discrete action tokens (pick vs. place vs. push), end-effector poses and forces, or even gripper commands. π₀.₅ typically uses continuous action representations for smooth, differentiable robot control.

### Pre-training and Transfer Learning

π₀.₅'s power comes from pre-training on diverse, large-scale robot data. Physical Intelligence collected trajectories from multiple robot embodiments (Franka Panda, mobile manipulators, quadrupeds), diverse tasks (pick, place, insert, poke, push, wipe, stack, sort), and multiple environments. This diversity prevents the model from overfitting to specific morphologies or task distributions. During pre-training, the model learns general skills: reaching movements transfer across robots, force estimation works on any arm with sensors, visual recognition of objects generalizes.

When fine-tuning π₀.₅ on a new task (e.g., our ball-in-cup task), we leverage this pre-training. The vision encoder has already learned to recognize objects, surfaces, and spatial relationships from internet-scale image data (via CLIP's pre-training). The language encoder understands semantic relationships ("gently" implies low force, "quickly" implies high speed) from text data. The fusion layers, trained on diverse robot data, have learned common patterns (to push something forward, increase force in that direction). Our fine-tuning then specializes these general skills to our specific task: "when you see a ball and a cup in this particular scene, and you read 'push gently,' output joint targets that successfully place the ball in the cup."

This transfer learning is crucial for data efficiency. A model trained from scratch on 300 ball-in-cup demonstrations would likely overfit (memorize specific positions and trajectories). Pre-trained π₀.₅, fine-tuned on the same 300 trajectories, learns to generalize because the pre-trained components already know what balls, cups, and pushing motions look like.

### Why π₀.₅ for Ball-in-Cup?

**Multimodal grounding:** Ball-in-cup requires the model to understand the scene (where is the ball? where is the cup? what's in between?), understand the task intent from language ("push gently" means careful, controlled motion), and output appropriate motor commands. π₀.₅'s multimodal architecture directly addresses this joint reasoning. A vision-only model might miss the language nuance (gentle vs. forceful); a language-only model might not understand spatial relationships. π₀.₅ fuses all three.

**Language conditioning for behavior modulation:** Language is not merely a task selector (pick vs. place); it modulates behavior within a task. "Push gently" and "push forcefully" are the same high-level action (push) with different execution parameters (force, speed, duration). π₀.₅'s learned embeddings can capture these correlations if trained on diverse examples. We validate this hypothesis by collecting data across language variants and measuring whether the learned model produces appropriately different force profiles.

**Pre-trained generalization:** Ball-in-cup has natural generalization challenges: ball size varies, cup location varies, surface properties vary. Pre-trained vision encoders (from CLIP, large-scale visual recognition) have learned to categorize and recognize diverse object shapes and materials, providing inductive biases that help generalize across these variations. A randomly initialized model would need much more data to learn these inductive biases.

**Scalability to more tasks:** Once we validate π₀.₅ on ball-in-cup, the same model (with different fine-tuning) can be applied to pick-and-place, insertion, wiping, and other manipulation tasks. The pre-trained components are task-agnostic; fine-tuning specializes them. This amortizes the cost of pre-training across many downstream tasks.

### Comparison to Alternatives

**Why not classical control + hand-coding?** We use hand-coded controllers to generate data, but not as the final solution. Hand-coded controllers require expertise (tuning PD gains, designing FSMs), don't generalize across objects or scenarios, and don't leverage language. π₀.₅ is learned and can generalize.

**Why not a simpler deep learning model (CNN + MLP)?** A simple convolutional network followed by dense layers could work for ball-in-cup, but would lack the inductive biases for multimodal reasoning and would need more data. Transformers (which π₀.₅ uses) have proven more sample-efficient and generalizable in vision and language tasks.

**Why not RL (Q-learning, policy gradients)?** RL directly optimizes task rewards (success in placing the ball) without requiring expert demonstrations. However, RL typically needs orders of magnitude more data (thousands to millions of trials) to learn from scratch. By using imitation learning with good demonstrations, we reduce data requirements 10–100×. RL is a natural next step (fine-tune π₀.₅ with reinforcement learning once behavioral cloning converges).

**Why not large language models (LLMs) alone?** LLMs (GPT, PaLM) are excellent at language reasoning but operate on text and images, not robot actions. VLA models like π₀.₅ specifically bridge this gap by grounding language understanding in robot embodiment: they learn what "pushing gently" means by observing actual robot pushing with force feedback. This embodied understanding cannot be learned from text alone.

### π₀.₅ Limitations and Mitigation

π₀.₅ is a powerful foundation model, but has limitations we must address. First, it is pre-trained on simulation and real robot data; our sim-to-real transfer will depend on the overlap between its training distribution and our specific task. Second, π₀.₅ operates on sequences of images and past proprioceptive state, without explicit 3D spatial models or physics understanding—it learns these implicitly. Third, the base model is large (billions of parameters) and computationally expensive to fine-tune; we mitigate this by using a smaller, task-specific variant during fine-tuning.

We address these limitations through our experimental design: we validate π₀.₅ thoroughly in simulation (Experiment 3–6), measure generalization (Experiment 4–5), and explicitly test language conditioning (Experiment 5). The sim-to-real gap is tackled in Phase 5 of our plan through domain randomization and eventual real-robot fine-tuning.
